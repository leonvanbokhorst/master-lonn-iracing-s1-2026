# Rudskogen Motorsenter

Rudskogen opened in 1990 as Norway’s first permanent asphalt circuit, tucked into the pine forests south-east of Oslo.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudskogen">Rudskogen — Wikipedia</a></sup> When the Norwegian government anointed it the country’s national motorsport facility in 2006, Hermann Tilke reshaped the property into today’s 3.254 km rollercoaster: 14 corners, a 640-metre straight and a 42-metre climb that crests in the trees before plunging downhill again.<sup><a href="https://www.racingcircuits.info/europe/norway/rudskogen.html">RacingCircuits.info — Rudskogen</a></sup> Modern STCC, GT and Porsche Sprint weekends share the tarmac with superkarts and police-training days, giving the track a Scandinavian dual personality—equal parts high-speed theatre and pragmatic driving school.

That duality defines the lap. Rudskogen strings together long, throttle-hungry arcs where bravery counts—especially the uphill Turn 3 sweeper and the downhill braking fight into Turn 6—yet it sneaks in late-apex kinks that punish anyone who forgets weight transfer. The forest lining acts like a tunnel, narrowing sightlines and forcing you to memorize reference points carved into rock faces and marshal posts. When autumn rain or Nordic dusk rolls in, the asphalt’s cambers become skating rinks, which is why locals obsess over rotation on corner entry instead of chasing mid-corner speed.

In iRacing the Ray FF1600 turns Rudskogen into a momentum clinic. The car never hits the limiter on the back straight unless you’re clinical through the preceding double-apex right, so the reward for clean exits is enormous. Expect to short-shift to third early to keep the rear planted on crests; excessive revs only adds wheelspin on the downhill launches. Because setup freedom is limited, your main tuning knob is brake bias—running 1–2 clicks forward keeps the nose secure when trail braking into Turn 6, while inching rearward helps rotate through the fast Turn 10 kink once tires are up to temp.

A productive practice recipe starts with sector work: hotlap the opening complex until you can land three consecutive laps within two tenths, then focus solely on braking repeatability at Turns 6 and 13 by using lap delta overlays. Graduate to 15-lap race runs that mimic tire fade and force you to manage dirty air in the medium-speed bowl of Turns 8–11. Finally, rehearse rolling starts from the grid slot halfway up the hill—the car loves to bog if you release the clutch at peak revs, and the slope exaggerates it. Nail that choreography and Rudskogen shifts from intimidating forest rally stage to a high-speed flow state.
